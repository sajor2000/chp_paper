Co-Author Brief · Chicago Health Map

A block-level view of neighborhood health that big-area maps can’t show

Our question: Does the Chicago Health Map’s block-level picture of chronic disease reveal patterns that get lost in the larger-area maps communities use today?

Target: JAMA Health Forum Focus: the City of Chicago Conditions: diabetes · COPD · high blood pressure
📝
Draft for our discussion. The numbers here are early and for the team only. We describe what the maps show — we are not claiming one map is “the truth,” that any neighborhood is doing something wrong, or that a difference means missed or unmet care.

1 Why this matters

Chicago has up to a 30-year difference in life expectancy from one neighborhood to the next. But the health maps people rely on — the city’s 77-neighborhood summaries and ZIP-code reports — average everything across a large area. One number can hide very different blocks inside it.

The Chicago Health Map shows health at a much smaller scale — the census tract, roughly a few city blocks. We want to show that this closer-in view surfaces real differences within a neighborhood that the bigger maps smooth over, so residents and local organizations can ask sharper questions about their own streets.

2 What we did

  • Put the Chicago Health Map’s block-level numbers side by side with the two health maps people use today.
  • Focused on three conditions that shape the life-expectancy gap and appear in every map: diabetes, COPD, and high blood pressure.
  • Measured a simple thing: how much block-to-block difference disappears when you zoom out from tracts to the big 77-neighborhood view — and whether the block-level map and the national survey map point in the same direction.

Early finding (team only): for diabetes and COPD, about half of the block-to-block differences are hidden inside the big-neighborhood averages. For high blood pressure it’s much less. That’s why we lead the story with diabetes and COPD.

3 The data

What is the Chicago Health Map?

It is built from real medical records, not a survey. Seven Chicago-area health systems share de-identified records, and for each small area we count the adult patients with a given diagnosis and divide by the adults seen there. So it shows how common a diagnosis is among the patients we can see in that area.

The seven systems: Rush, Northwestern Medicine, UChicago Medicine, UI Health, Cook County Health, AllianceChicago (community health centers), and Endeavor Health — about 2.8 million adults over 2019–2024. Because it counts patients who were seen (not everyone who lives there), it is a picture of who comes for care, not a count of every case, and how complete that picture is varies from area to area.

Which places count as “Chicago”?

One thing to keep straight. The Chicago Health Map records cover the whole six-county Chicago region. For this paper we look only at the City of Chicago, at two zoom levels:

Close-in view
866 census tracts
Each is roughly a few city blocks (about 1,200–8,000 people).
Zoomed-out view
77 neighborhoods
Chicago’s well-known community areas (about 35,000 people each).

The Chicago Health Map gives us both views, which is exactly what lets us measure how much detail is lost when we zoom out. (If they can be built cleanly, ZIP groupings serve as an extra check that the story doesn’t depend on where the map lines fall.)

Trustworthy by design: every number is locked to a dated copy of its source and can be reproduced exactly, and small counts are hidden to protect privacy — the standard expected for publishing health-system data.

4 Our analysis

The methods (plain-English gloss on the right). All are descriptive comparisons — no outcome modeling.

MethodWhat it tells us
Variance partitioning — a two-level model (tracts nested in community areas) reporting the variance partition coefficient (VPC/ICC), plus discriminatory accuracy (AUC)How much block-to-block variation sits within a neighborhood vs. between neighborhoods; and how well the neighborhood label alone predicts a given block
Concordance & agreement vs. CDC PLACES — Spearman rank correlation (primary), quartile agreement via weighted kappa and Gwet’s AC1, with uncertainty propagated (Monte-Carlo from PLACES confidence intervals + ACS margins of error)Whether the two maps rank areas the same way, and where they genuinely diverge rather than differ by chance
Scale sensitivity (modifiable areal unit) — the same metrics recomputed at the tract and community-area levels (and ZIP groupings if they can be built cleanly from the source records)How much the story depends on which map lines you use
Spatial clustering — local Moran’s I (LISA) and Getis-Ord Gi*, bivariate LISA (Health Map × PLACES), and a spatial scan statistic, with false-discovery-rate controlWhere hotspots stand out at the block level and blur away when averaged into a big neighborhood

5 Questions for Ashley

The methodological forks where your call matters most — each is a genuine choice.

Variance partitioning

We want a defensible headline for how much block-to-block variation lives within neighborhoods.

Your call: a two-level MAIHDA / VPC-ICC model with discriminatory accuracy (AUC), or a random-effects variance-components decomposition? With only ~77 upper-level units, is MAIHDA’s precision adequate, and do you want empirical-Bayes shrinkage on the within-area estimates? How should we handle tracts that straddle neighborhood boundaries — dominant assignment vs. areal weights — and report bootstrap CIs?

Agreement metrics vs. PLACES

The two sources are on different scales (patients-seen vs. modeled population), and high blood pressure has skewed marginals that will trip the kappa paradox.

Your call: add Gwet’s AC1/AC2 alongside weighted kappa? Is Lin’s concordance correlation misleading here given the different estimands (so we keep Spearman primary)? For uncertainty, Monte-Carlo propagation from PLACES CIs + ACS MOEs, or an analytic approximation?

Spatial clustering & multiplicity

We plan tract-level LISA/Gi*, bivariate LISA, and a spatial scan across three conditions.

Your call: weight specification (queen contiguity vs. distance band)? How to control multiplicity across many local cluster tests (FDR within a named family)? SaTScan vs. a flexible-shape scan? And how to present the scale-sensitivity results without implying any one geography is the “true” one?

6 Why it helps — and what it can’t say

  • For community health centers and local groups: a street-level view to see where to look and to compare against what they already know.
  • For researchers and the health department: a reproducible, close-in complement to the survey maps.
  • What it can’t say: it describes neighborhoods, not individual people; it counts diagnosed patients, not everyone; coverage varies by area; and a difference between maps is a difference in measurement, not proof that anyone is being missed or under-served.
