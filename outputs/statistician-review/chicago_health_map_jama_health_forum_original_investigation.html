Key Points

Question:

When Chicago Health Map tract estimates are replaced by community-area summaries, how much information about electronic health record–diagnosed proportions is no longer visible?

Findings:

In this ecological study, community-area summaries changed quartile classification for 33.8% of 720 hypertension tracts and 52.4% of 401 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease tracts. Within-community variation accounted for 19.5% and 46.9% of observed tract variation, respectively.

Meaning:

Chicago Health Map can supplement population surveillance by showing clinically observed small-area variation within familiar reporting units. Its estimates do not represent all residents and cannot establish community need.

Abstract

Importance

Chicago Health Map displays aggregate multisystem electronic health record diagnosis data by census tract and larger geographic units. Its value depends on whether tract estimates add information beyond larger reporting areas and retain clear health-system denominator limits.

Objective

To describe Chicago Health Map and demonstrate a geographic-resolution use case by quantifying tract-level information not retained by leave-one-tract-out community-area summaries.

Design

Ecological repeated cross-sectional study of aggregate records from 2019 through 2024, with primary analyses pooling 2022 through 2024.

Setting

Chicago, Illinois.

Participants

Adults observed in participating Chicago Area Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Network systems. The annual denominator used source-defined adults with at least 1 of 39 conditions. The study team reports annual cross-system deduplication, which could not be independently verified from aggregate files.

Exposures

Crude tract-level electronic health record–diagnosed proportions for hypertension and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease.

Main Outcomes and Measures

Outcomes were within-community variance share, median absolute percentile-rank difference, quartile disagreement, and highest-quartile movement. Each tract was compared with the pooled estimate from other eligible tracts in its community area. Community-area cluster bootstraps provided percentile 95% CIs.

Results

Chicago Health Map included 1848 community-area condition-year records. The primary analysis included 720 hypertension tracts and 401 chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) tracts. For hypertension, within-community variance share was 19.5% (95% CI, 15.1%-27.6%). Median absolute rank difference was 0.085 (95% CI, 0.080-0.104), quartile disagreement was 33.8% (95% CI, 28.9%-43.4%), and highest-quartile movement was 11.7% (95% CI, 6.7%-20.0%). Corresponding COPD estimates were 46.9% (95% CI, 33.3%-62.1%), 0.145 (95% CI, 0.125-0.173), 52.4% (95% CI, 47.3%-61.6%), and 23.4% (95% CI, 18.1%-33.8%).

Conclusions and Relevance

Community-area summaries did not retain all tract-level variation. Chicago Health Map can identify small-area questions for follow-up with population surveillance and local evidence. It should supplement, not replace, resident-population data.

Introduction

Local public health agencies need geographically specific information to monitor chronic disease and guide investigation. Surveys and modeled estimates provide a population frame, but sample size, release schedules, and privacy protections can limit neighborhood comparisons. Electronic health records (EHRs) can add timely clinical observations and support chronic disease surveillance, small-area estimation, and multisite reporting.1-5 Their interpretation must account for selection through health care access, utilization, participating institutions, documentation, and data linkage.6-12

This distinction matters within cities. EHR measures describe diagnoses among people observed in care, whereas surveys and modeled estimates target resident populations.3-4,7,13-15 Neither source is a gold standard for the other. Health-system data can show where clinically recorded patterns vary within public reporting units and identify questions for examination with population-based evidence.

Chicago Health Map is a public interactive website maintained by the Rush Health Equity Data Analytics Studio. It displays annual aggregate diagnosis percentages from the Chicago Area Patient-Centered Outcomes Research Network (CAPriCORN) for 38 chronic conditions and firearm injury across 6 counties in metropolitan Chicago. Visitors can select a condition, year, demographic group, and geographic unit. A shaded map presents the percentage diagnosed, identifies areas with insufficient data, and provides reliability information based on patient coverage and demographic alignment. Census tracts provide the most detailed view, and the website also presents Chicago’s 77 community areas. These percentages describe recorded diagnoses among adults observed in participating systems, not disease prevalence among all residents.16-18

We describe the public website, its data sources, and its interpretation limits, then evaluate one scientific use case. Because the website displays the same condition at different geographic resolutions, we asked how much tract-level information is no longer visible when validated tract counts are summarized at the community-area level. We hypothesized that tract estimates would show within-community variation for hypertension and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). The prespecified interpretation excluded population prevalence, causal effects, and evidence of community need.

Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

We conducted an ecological repeated cross-sectional study of aggregate Chicago Health Map records from 2019 through 2024. The primary geographic-resolution analysis pooled 2022 through 2024, years selected to provide a contemporary 3-year period with consistent source fields. CAPriCORN supplied the clinical network context. American Community Survey data supplied area covariates for supplementary models. City of Chicago and US Census Bureau files supplied geographic boundaries and tract relationships. CDC PLACES, Healthy Chicago Survey, and Chicago Health Atlas data supported secondary cross-source analyses.16-22 Reporting followed STROBE and RECORD guidance.23-24

Chicago Health Map Website and Data Resource

ChicagoHealthMap.com presents a public choropleth map of aggregate diagnosis percentages. Visitors can select a condition and geographic unit, filter by year, age, race and ethnicity, and gender, and inspect areas labeled as having insufficient data. The website also displays source-provided reliability information and optional contextual layers, including social vulnerability and neighborhood resources. The clinical source period spans 2019 through 2024 across Cook, DuPage, Lake, Will, Kane, and McHenry Counties. Census tracts are the primary small-area unit. Within Chicago, the website also presents the city’s 77 community areas.16,18

The public resource combines CAPriCORN diagnosis aggregates with American Community Survey population and demographic estimates, US Census Bureau TIGER tract boundaries, and Chicago Data Portal community-area boundaries. Its contextual layers draw on the CDC and Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry Social Vulnerability Index, OpenStreetMap, Chicago Police Department records, and City of Chicago 311 data. CAPriCORN contributors standardize structured records with the PCORnet Common Data Model. Diagnoses are defined from International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes. Values based on fewer than 10 people are suppressed, and reliability flags summarize patient capture and demographic alignment.17-18

This study used the governed aggregate exports underlying the website rather than values transcribed from the map. We restricted the analysis to Chicago and compared the census tract view with community-area summaries constructed from the same validated tract numerators and denominators. The primary analysis used CAPriCORN diagnosis aggregates and geographic crosswalks. American Community Survey variables entered only supplementary models. The website’s retail, service, crime, and social vulnerability layers were not analyzed. The study evaluated the geographic information provided by the underlying data. It did not evaluate website usability, community engagement, or whether viewing the map changed policy or resource decisions.

The analytic unit was a geography-condition-year aggregate. No patient-level records were used. For each tract and condition, the numerator was the annual source-defined count of adults with the diagnosis, and the denominator was the annual source-defined count of adults with at least 1 of 39 included conditions who were observed that year. The study team reports that the upstream data integration process deduplicated adults across systems within each year. The aggregate files did not permit independent verification of person-level linkage or identification of people appearing in multiple years. We therefore refer to every estimate as an EHR-diagnosed proportion among observed CAPriCORN adults, not population prevalence.

Eligibility and Geographic Linkage

Tracts had to fall within the frozen Chicago boundary definition, have a denominator of at least 30, have an observable numerator and nonsuppressed estimate, and contribute all 3 primary years. We assigned each tract to its dominant community area only when the maximum overlay weight was at least 0.99. The leave-one-out comparator also required another eligible tract in that community area. These rules yielded 777 complete hypertension tracts and 448 complete COPD tracts before dominant-area linkage, 722 and 411 after linkage, and 720 and 401 with a leave-one-out comparator.

We treated unresolved zero-or-suppressed values as informative missingness. The primary analysis used complete cases. Scenario analyses replaced an unresolved numerator with 0 or with the smaller of 9 and the denominator. These scenarios were not interpreted as formal bounds. Age-standardized estimates were not computed. Stored age-stratum fields were present, but their sums rarely matched the all-age totals, their suppression semantics were undocumented, and no compatible standard population had been approved.

Primary Estimands

We summed annual numerators and denominators before division. For focal tract i in community area j, the pooled tract estimate was pij = ∑t yijt / ∑t nijt. Its community-area comparator excluded the focal tract: p−i,j = ∑k ≠ i,t ykjt / ∑k ≠ i,t nkjt. Tests prevented substitution of an unweighted mean of annual percentages and verified that each focal tract contributed neither numerator nor denominator to its comparator.

We estimated 4 complementary outcomes. First, the within-community variance share was the observed-scale within-area component divided by total tract variance using a one-way method-of-moments partition.25-26 Second, we calculated the median absolute difference between empirical tract and leave-one-out comparator percentile ranks. Third, we measured disagreement across quartiles defined by fixed percentile boundaries at 0.25, 0.50, and 0.75, using average ranks for ties. Fourth, we measured movement into or out of the highest quartile. No magnitude threshold was defined after observing the results.

Uncertainty and Sensitivity Analyses

We resampled community areas with replacement, retained their member tracts, and recomputed ranks and categories within each replicate. The primary analysis used 1000 replicates, seed 20260715, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles.27-29 We used the same design for all 4 primary estimands.

To quantify finite-denominator variation, we ran 1000 binomial simulations using each tract-year denominator and its leave-one-out area probability. This simulation was a noise reference, not a measurement-error correction. We also applied beta-binomial empirical-Bayes shrinkage as a sensitivity, while retaining unshrunk estimates as primary.30 Source-published community-area values and Census ZIP Code Tabulation Area values were secondary because their weighting and condition-specific denominators were not fully documented. Cross-source Spearman correlation and quadratic weighted kappa described alignment without treating either source as correct.31

Supplementary community-area models used ordinary least squares with HC3 covariance, residual-degrees-of-freedom (t) critical values, and 95% CIs.32 The locked adjustment set was percentage aged 65 years or older, percentage female, percentage below the federal poverty level, and mean 2022 through 2024 capture. Regression-residual Moran I used row-standardized queen weights and 9999 fitted-design null simulations. Spatial error models were sensitivities.33-35 Combined diabetes was not analyzed because mutual exclusivity and denominator equivalence were not established. We applied NCHS reliability principles cautiously because they were developed for survey estimates rather than EHR denominators.36

Analyses used Python 3.13.14 (Python Software Foundation) with pandas 3.0.3, NumPy 2.5.1, SciPy 1.18.0, statsmodels 0.14.6, and Matplotlib 3.11.0. The review manuscript was rendered in R 4.6.1 (R Foundation). All CIs were 2-sided 95% intervals.

Results

Data Availability and Cohort Flow

The community-area resource contained 1848 condition-year records across 77 community areas and 6 years (Table 1). Hypertension records were complete across all 462 area-years. 5 of 462 COPD area-year records (1.1%) were suppressed. The source-published median condition-record denominator was 3,193 for hypertension and 3,163 for COPD. These denominators were geography-period-condition observations, not counts of unique people across the study period.

Among 782 Chicago-boundary tracts for hypertension, 777 had complete 2022 through 2024 data, 722 met the dominant-area criterion, and 720 had another eligible tract in the area. For COPD, the corresponding counts were 782, 448, 411, and 401 (Figure 1). The greater COPD attrition reflected unresolved or suppressed values and tracts without a focal-condition source row, not exclusion from the common Chicago boundary.

For COPD, the 401 included tracts had a median annual denominator of 409, compared with 289 among the 381 excluded tracts. Excluded COPD tracts contributed 779 unresolved or ineligible tract-year records. The COPD results therefore describe a more selected tract sample than the hypertension results. These aggregate comparisons do not identify the direction or magnitude of selection bias.

Primary Geographic-Resolution Estimates

For hypertension, within-community variation accounted for 19.5% (95% CI, 15.1%-27.6%) of observed tract variation (Table 2). The median absolute percentile-rank difference was 0.085 (95% CI, 0.080-0.104). Community-area summaries assigned 33.8% (95% CI, 28.9%-43.4%) of tracts to a different quartile and changed highest-quartile status for 11.7% (95% CI, 6.7%-20.0%).

For COPD, within-community variation accounted for 46.9% (95% CI, 33.3%-62.1%) of tract variation. The median absolute rank difference was 0.145 (95% CI, 0.125-0.173). Quartile disagreement affected 52.4% (95% CI, 47.3%-61.6%) of tracts, and highest-quartile status changed for 23.4% (95% CI, 18.1%-33.8%).

Exploratory AUC analyses are reported only in the statistician review companion, RMD_chm_paper_r_Ashley.html.

Sensitivity Analyses

For the noise comparison, all 4 metrics were recomputed in the leave-one-tract-out simulation frame of 720 hypertension tracts and 401 COPD tracts. The variance-share noise contrast therefore used a narrower population than the primary variance-share estimate. For hypertension, observed minus noise-only median differences were 15.9 percentage points for within-community variance share, 0.038 percentile-rank units, 19.0 percentage points for quartile disagreement, and 6.1 percentage points for highest-quartile movement. The corresponding COPD differences were 31.5 percentage points, 0.060, 15.7 percentage points, and 7.0 percentage points. These contrasts describe excess over the simulation median and are not bias-corrected estimates. Empirical-Bayes shrinkage produced quartile-disagreement estimates of 33.5% for hypertension and 52.1% for COPD. The primary estimate and finite-denominator review graphics are reported in the statistician review companion.

Suppression scenarios did not change hypertension estimates. For COPD, imputing unresolved numerators as 0 produced 48.9% quartile disagreement, whereas assigning up to 9 produced 52.2%. Corresponding highest-quartile movement was 16.8% and 16.5%. These scenarios included 713 tracts and therefore changed both the values and the analytic population. Source-published community-area, noncrossing-tract, ZCTA, reliability-stratified, cross-source, and annual analyses are reported in the statistician review companion. They were treated as sensitivities rather than independent standards.

Discussion

In this worked use case of Chicago Health Map, community-area summaries retained broad geographic signal but did not retain all clinically observed tract variation. The estimated magnitudes differed between COPD and hypertension, but the study did not prespecify or estimate between-condition contrasts. Empirical-Bayes shrinkage and finite-denominator simulations tested how much the result depended on unstable crude proportions. The simulation contrasts were descriptive and did not remove measurement error. These results support a supplementary role for small-area health-system data: they can identify variation within common public reporting units that merits investigation with population-based and community evidence.

The results do not imply that tract estimates are more accurate than community-area estimates. The leave-one-out design answered a narrower question by comparing each tract with a coarser summary constructed from other tracts in the same source frame. This avoided allowing the focal tract to define its own comparator and avoided treating source-published community-area values as a gold standard. Prior EHR surveillance studies have similarly shown that routine clinical data can provide timely neighborhood information while remaining sensitive to patient capture, phenotype definitions, participating systems, and utilization.7-15,37-44

COPD and hypertension differed in data completeness and estimated information loss. COPD had more unresolved or suppressed tract-years, fewer complete tracts, and wider uncertainty. Its tract-community differences therefore coexist with a more selected analytic frame. Hypertension had near-complete tract availability and also showed within-area differences. The study did not assign a threshold for practical importance, test a between-condition contrast, or establish where services are needed. A tract flagged by this analysis is a place where the EHR-derived description differs from its area’s summary, not evidence of underdiagnosis, poor care, disease burden, or an intervention target.

The noise simulation addressed one source of measurement uncertainty. The observed-minus-simulation contrasts quantified how far each primary metric lay above or below the noise-only median. The simulation assumed binomial variation around the leave-one-out area probability and did not represent diagnostic misclassification, health-system selection, extra-binomial variation, geocoding error, spatial dependence, cross-year dependence, or changes in participating institutions. Shrinkage stabilized crude proportions under a beta-binomial model but could not correct the EHR population to the resident population.

Chicago Health Map can complement public health data when each source retains its own inferential boundary. Population surveys, vital records, and modeled surveillance remain necessary for resident-population inference. Health-system records can add a clinically grounded tract-level signal and can be refreshed on different schedules. Used together, the sources can identify discordant areas, generate hypotheses about ascertainment or access, and support targeted data-quality review. They should not be combined as if they measure the same population, and EHR estimates should not independently direct resource allocation. The same principle underlies multisite EHR surveillance networks and health information exchange programs.1,9,12,14,40-44

The website supports several bounded, practical uses. A public health team can use the tract view to determine whether a community-area average conceals smaller-area differences, then examine those differences with population surveillance and local knowledge. A health system can review a diagnosis pattern beside capture and reliability information to identify places that require data-quality or ascertainment review. A community organization can use a community snapshot to frame questions for local partners and request more specific evidence. These examples concern question generation and measurement review. This study did not test whether the website changed programs, resource allocation, or health outcomes.

Limitations

The denominator included adults observed in participating systems who had at least 1 of 39 conditions. It did not represent all Chicago residents, all health care users, or all adults with the focal condition. The study team reports that annual cross-system deduplication was performed upstream, but the aggregate files could not verify linkage or identify people observed in multiple years.

The measures were crude. Age fields were present, but they did not additively reconcile with the all-age totals and their suppression semantics were undocumented. Available aggregate data therefore did not support defensible age standardization, and differences in tract composition could contribute to the observed variation. Unresolved and suppressed records were informative, particularly for COPD. The scenario analyses changed the analytic population and were not formal bounds.

Geographic assignment depended on geocoding, boundary vintages, and the 0.99 dominant-area rule. Results may differ with other neighborhood definitions. Reliability categories were supplied by the source. The relationship between the internal 5-tier labels and the public 4-tier framework still requires source-owner confirmation.

The study evaluated 2 conditions in 1 metropolitan network without temporal holdout or external replication. Ecological findings cannot be interpreted for individuals or as causal effects. The study evaluated the aggregate data underlying the website. It did not test whether the website changed understanding, decisions, resource allocation, or health outcomes.

Conclusions

Tract-level Chicago Health Map measures contained clinically observed geographic information that leave-one-out community-area summaries did not fully retain. The findings support using health-system research data to supplement public health surveillance and generate small-area questions, while preserving population-based data as the basis for community inference and action.
